# House style, derived from edits rather than from taste

This exists because a draft came back with the note *"the voice is absolutely
gone."* It was right. Everything below was extracted from one marked-up pass
over `ARTICLE.md`, by diffing what was written against what survived. The
rules are descriptive, not aspirational: each one is a thing that was actually
cut or actually added.

Read this before writing anything with a byline on it.

## The four habits that got cut

**1. Chopped declaratives for punch.** Short sentences hammered in sequence
read as terse, not as forceful.

> written: Nobody ships it on purpose. It shipped here because it passed its tests.
> kept:    Nobody ships it on purpose, it shipped here because it passed its tests.

The comma splice is deliberate and it is fine. Let sentences run into each
other the way speech does. If three sentences in a row are under ten words,
that is a signal, not a style.

**2. The confession-then-reveal set piece.** A little admission of fault,
then a bolded turn, then an aphorism to close the paragraph. Once in an
article is voice. Six times is a tic, and every instance after the first got
deleted.

> written: We came into this literature late, and most of what we assumed was
>          ours had already been done by somebody else. **Somebody already
>          built this benchmark.**
> kept:    **The benchmark already exists.**

Say the fact. The reader does not need to watch the realisation happen.

**3. Impersonal formal openers.** "The question this project set out to
answer is how often that happens" became "We wanted to look at cases like
this." Write what a person would say out loud, not what a paper would say.

**4. Paragraphs that end on a button.** Not every paragraph needs a closing
line that lands. Most should just stop when the point is made.

## The four things that got added

**1. Opinion, unhedged.** The equivalence library "is an excellent piece of
software in our estimation and deserves lots of love." Nothing in the data
says that. Say it anyway. Be generous about other people's work by name.

**2. Interest, out loud.** "The really nice thing about this is that none of
it needs a single API call." "The interesting thing is that they are
basically ordinary things." Tell the reader which parts you enjoyed.

**3. Stakes you can picture.** Not "at the point of use" but "on a system
that is failing at 5 am." One concrete image beats three abstract clauses.

**4. Speculation, clearly marked.** A whole paragraph guessing at why a
content filter behaves oddly, hedged with "we think maybe" and a parenthetical
admitting it is off-topic. Unresolved is allowed. Curious is allowed. The
register is a smart colleague thinking out loud, not a committee.

## Mechanics

- Headings are lowercase. `## what we did`, not `## What We Did`.
- Em-dashes are rationed. Prefer a comma, or a full stop, or brackets.
- "rather than" is a crutch. So is "which is the point," "that is the
  finding," and any sentence whose job is to tell the reader what they just
  read.
- Hedges that sound human are good: "basically," "surprise, surprise," "yes,
  we ran even more benchmarks," "at least as of late 2026."
- Callbacks are good. "Ground truth continues to be hard" pays off a heading
  from six sections earlier.
- Length is not the enemy. The edited version was *shorter* overall while
  having *longer* sentences. Cut structure, not texture.

## The test

Read it aloud. If it sounds like a person who did the work explaining it to
someone they respect, it passes. If it sounds like the abstract of a paper, or
like a LinkedIn post that has learned about antithesis, start again.
